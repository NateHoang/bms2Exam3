    • Clinical Forms of Candidiasis

    Form of Candidiasis Clinical Presentation Key Diagnostic / Clinical Feature
    Pseudomembranous
    • "Curdled milk" or "cottage cheese" white plaques.
    • Can be scraped off with a tongue blade.
    Acute Erythematous
    • Painful, red, denuded patches on the mucosa.
    • Often occurs following broad-spectrum antibiotic therapy such as steroid inhaler.
    • Antibiotics kill competing bacteria, allowing Candida overgrowth.
    Chronic Hyperplastic
    • Persistent bilateral white patches or plaques on buccal commissure regions
    • Observed in smokers and high alcohol consumers.
    • Cannot be scraped off.
    • Carries a higher risk of transforming into oral cancer.
    Chronic Erythematous (Denture Stomatitis)
    • Redness and inflammation limited to the area under a denture.
    • Most common on the palate.
    • Caused by ill-fitting dentures, or inadequate oral hygiene
    • No invasion of underlying epithelium.
    Angular Cheilitis
    • Redness, cracking, and crusting at the corners of the mouth.
    • Often a co-infection of Candida and bacteria such as Staph aureus or Streptococcus.
    Median Rhomboid Glossitis
    • Flat or raised red area of the filiform papillae with a red area in the central dorsal tongue.
    • Highly associated with smoking.
    • Highly associated with the use of inhaled steroids.
    • Kissing Lesion may develop on on hard palate
    Chronic Mucocutaneous
    • Severe, persistent infections of the skin, nails, and mucosa.
    • Impaired cellular immunity against Candida.
    • Endocrine disorders

    Important Virulence Factors of C. albicans

    • Adhesion: Uses adhesins to stick to host cells and dental materials, such as dentures.
    • Phenotypic Switching: The ability to switch between yeast for spread and hyphae for invading tissue.
    • Enzymes: Produces aspartyl proteinases and phospholipases that digest host cell membranes.

    Section 7: Phagocytosis Process

    Step Description
    1. Attachment Bacterium becomes attached to membrane evaginations called pseudopodia
    2. Ingestion Bacterium is ingested, forming a phagosome
    3. Fusion Phagosome fuses with lysosome → phagolysosome
    4. Killing & Digestion
    • Bacterium killed by antimicrobial agents (defensins)
    • Low pH
    • Hydrolytic enzymes (proteases)
    • Oxidative burst (ROS, nitric oxide)
    5. Release Digestion products released from cell
    Activation: Direct Binding PAMPs bind directly to PRRs on phagocytic cells → also activates cytokine production
    Activation: Indirect (Opsonization) Opsonins (IgG, C3b) coat microbe → recognized by opsonin receptors on phagocytes → activates phagocytosis

    🧪 Generation of Receptor Diversity: V(D)J Recombination

    Component Details
    Goal
  • Generate diverse lymphocyte set, each with unique antigen-specific receptor
  • Destroy self-reactive lymphocytes
  • Preserve non-self-reactive lymphocytes for maturation in secondary lymphoid organs
    • Mechanism Recombination of Variable (V) region gene segments with Diversity (D) and Joining (J) gene segments
    Key Enzymes RAG1 and RAG2 recombination activating genes encode V(D)J recombinase enzyme complex
    Chromosome Heavy chain located on Chromosome 14
    Diversity Types
    • Combinatorial diversity: Use of different V, D, J segments
    • Junctional diversity: Different N and P nucleotides in each clone
    Outcome Millions of different recombined antigen receptor V genes

    Lecture 5: Inflammation

    Section 1: Inflammation — Overview

    Aspect Description
    Definition Protective response of vascularized tissues to insults
    Requirement Must have vascularized tissue
    Goal Eradicate the initial cause of cell injury, eliminate offending agents, and restore homeostasis to disrupted host tissues
    Mechanism Brings cells and molecules of host defense from circulation to the site of injury
    Regulation Must be tightly regulated; uncontrolled inflammation can cause chronic inflammatory disease

    Section 2: Causes of Inflammation

    Cause Examples
    Microbial infections
    • Bacterial, fungal, viral, or parasitic infections
    • Microbial toxins
    Injury
    • Ischemic: myocardial infarction
    • Chemical: environmental chemicals
    • Physical: burns, frostbite
    Foreign bodies
    • Splinters that breach physical barriers
    • May cause tissue injury or introduce microbes
    Immune reactions
    • Hypersensitivity reactions
    • Autoimmune reactions
    • Immunologic injury

    Section 3: Sequence of Inflammation: The 5 Rs

    Step Description
    1. Recognition Detection of the offending agent, including PAMPs and DAMPs
    2. Leukocyte Recruitment Cells migrate to the injury site
    3. Removal Destruction and elimination of the threat
    4. Regulation / Termination End the inflammatory process
    5. Tissue Repair Regain normal tissue or form fibrous tissue/scar

    Defects in Innate Immunity

    Immunodeficiency Classification Genetic Defect Immunopathogenesis Clinical Manifestations Key Features

    Therapeutic Approaches for Congenital Immunodeficiency

    Approach Details
    1. Minimize and control infections Treatment with Antiviral, antibacterial or antifungal
    2. Passive immunization with pooled antibodies is very beneficial for agammaglobulinemic patients.
    3. Hematopoietic stem cell transplantation is currently the treatment of choice for many immunodeficiency diseases and has been successful in the treatment of SCID. It is most successful with careful T cell depletion and HLA matching to prevent graft-versus-host disease.
    4. Enzyme replacement therapy Injection of the defective enzymes associated with immunodeficiency (E.g., RAG or Artemis SCID).
    5. Gene Therapy ideal therapy for congenital disorders of lymphocytes is to re-express the defective gene in self renewing stem cells. This approach can be done by isolating the patient's own HSCs in the lab and a normal copy of the gene is introduced in these cells via a viral vector capable of integrating itself in the host cell genome.

    Tolerance and Autoimmunity

    Mechanisms of Central and Peripheral Tolerance

    Tolerance Type Location Process on Self-Reactive Lymphocytes Key Outcomes
    Central tolerance Generative lymphoid organs: bone marrow (B cells) and thymus (T cells)
    • Immature self-reactive lymphocytes with high avidity tolerogen recognition are inactivated via:
    • Clonal deletion – lymphocyte death
    • Receptor editing – replacement of self-reactive antigen receptor with non-self-reactive receptor (B cells only)
    • Development into Treg cells (CD4+ T cells only)
    • Weak/no recognition of self-antigen → survival
    • Strong recognition → apoptosis, editing, or Treg generation
    Peripheral tolerance Peripheral tissues and lymphoid organs
    • Mature self-reactive lymphocytes inactivated by:
    • Suppression by regulatory T cells
    • Clonal anergy – functional unresponsiveness
    • Peripheral deletion – apoptosis
    		 Prevents activation against self-antigens in periphery

    Regulatory T Cells (Tregs)

    Feature Details
    Identifying markers CD4+ FoxP3+ CD25high – FoxP3 and CD25 are essential for Treg generation and function; defects in FoxP3 increase risk of autoimmune diseases
    Main function Maintain self-tolerance and suppress immune response; act on effector T cells, DCs, B cells, macrophages, NK cells
    Effector mechanisms
    • Suppressive cytokines – IL-10, TGFβ, IL-35
    • IL-2 consumption – via high CD25 expression, deprives effector T cells of IL-2 and induces Bim-mediated apoptosis
    • Cytolysis – granzyme-mediated apoptosis
    • Modulation of DC maturation – CTLA-4 (CD152) binds CD80/86, inducing tryptophan/kynurenine pathway leading to cell cycle arrest

    Mechanisms of Peripheral Deletion and Anergy

    Mechanism Description
    Anergy
  • Self-reactive B or T cell deprived of co-stimulatory signals
  • Engages co-inhibitory receptors instead
  • B cells lose IgM and keep unresponsive IgD
  • Cell does not die but becomes unresponsive
    		•
    Peripheral deletion Self-reactive T cells removed via apoptosis (intrinsic mitochondrial pathway or extrinsic death receptor pathway). Self-reactive B cells fail to receive T-cell help → apoptosis via intrinsic pathway

    Failure of Self-Tolerance: Autoimmune Diseases

    Aspect Details
    Definition Breakdown in self-tolerance → inability to distinguish self from non-self or misinterpretation of self-component as dangerous → immune attack on host tissues
    Etiology Genetic factors + Environmental factors
    Category Systemic (e.g., RA, MS) or organ-specific (e.g., type 1 diabetes) depending on distribution of autoantigens
    Course Most likely chronic and progressive; self-antigens are persistent, and amplification mechanisms perpetuate the response

    Genetic and Environmental Contributors

    Factor Details
    Susceptibility genes
    • HLA gene polymorphism – certain alleles linked with T1D, RA
    • Non-HLA genes – e.g., insulin gene in T1D
    Anatomic alterations Ischemic injury or trauma can expose normally concealed self-antigens
    Gender Many autoimmune diseases have higher incidence in females; hormones and sex-related immune differences implicated
    Environmental factors
    • Infections – viral or bacterial
    • Drugs or chemicals
    • Smoking and oxidative stress – increase severity
    Role of infections
    • Two principal mechanisms:
    • Bystander activation – T cells activated via cytokines in a TCR-independent manner, leading to activation of self-reactive T cells
    • Molecular mimicry – microbial antigens with high similarity to self-antigens
    • Streptococcus pyogenes → rheumatic fever (antibodies against N-acetylglucosamine cross-react with heart muscle)
    • EBV linked to SLE

    2. Histocompatibility & MHC

    Concept Details
    Histocompatibility Similarity in histocompatibility antigens between donor and recipient to facilitate successful transplantation
    Major influence Human leukocyte antigen (HLA) – also known as MHC (Major Histocompatibility Complex)
    Minor influence Minor histocompatibility antigens – small peptides on cell surface associated with MHC class I or II; role in fine-tuning transplantation success and in GVHD after HSCT
    MHC genes location Chromosome 6; highly polymorphic (many alleles per gene)
    HLA nomenclature Systemic, e.g., HLA-A02:101:01:02N* – fields indicate allele group, specific HLA protein, synonymous DNA substitution, and non-coding region differences; suffix denotes expression changes

    MHC Class I

    Feature Description
    Genes HLA-A, HLA-B, HLA-C
    Structure Pairs with β₂-microglobulin to form complete class I molecule
    Polymorphism ~1,500 gene isoforms encode the three classes
    Expression All nucleated cells
    Function Displays endogenous proteins (cellular, viral, tumor) for recognition by CD8⁺ T cells
    Antigen presentation Proteasome degrades proteins → peptides transported via TAP into ER → bind MHC I → Golgi → cell surface → CTL inspection

    MHC Class II

    Feature Description
    Genes HLA-DP, HLA-DQ, HLA-DR
    Structure Made of α and β polypeptide chains
    Polymorphism >700 different versions in humans
    Expression Antigen-presenting cells (APCs)
    Function Presents exogenous antigens to CD4⁺ T cells
    Why no endogenous display? Invariant chain blocks binding groove in ER → guides MHC II to endosome that fuses with phagosome → MHC II then exposed to exogenous antigens
    Regulation Cytokines increase MHC expression

    Clinical Significance of MHC

    Aspect Details
    MHC & autoimmune disease Association between MHC polymorphism and diseases: Type 1 diabetes (HLA-DR3), Rheumatoid arthritis (HLA-DRB1), Multiple sclerosis (HLA-DR2)
    MHC & transplantation High allotypic polymorphism of MHC molecules → plays major role in transplant rejection risk. Matching donors/recipients with highest MHC similarity → favorable success rate. Donor MHC antigens can cause rejection; donor T and B cells (antibodies) play role in GVHD
    Immune-privileged sites Brain, eye, testes, placenta, fetus – protected from natural immune responses. HLA matching not required (e.g., corneal transplant)

    4. Transplant Outcomes: Alloreactions

    Outcome Description
    Transplant rejection Recipient immune system attacks the graft (solid organs)
    Graft-vs-Host Disease (GVHD) Donor immune cells attack the recipient (mainly in HSCT)

    Transplant Rejection Patterns

    Pattern Timing / Key Features Mediators / Mechanism
    Hyperacute Rejection
    • Rare; occurs minutes to hours after vascular anastomosis
    • Characterized by thrombotic occlusion of graft vasculature
    • Preexisting IgM alloantibodies against ABO antigens
    • IgG developed from previous exposure (transplant, transfusion)
    • Natural antibodies in xenotransplantation
    • Circulating alloantigen-specific antibodies bind → complement activation → endothelial damage → inflammation → thrombosis
    Acute Rejection
    • Injury to graft parenchyma and blood vessels
    • May occur after reduction of immunosuppression
    • Mediated by alloreactive T cells and antibodies
    • Acute cellular rejection: CTL-mediated killing of parenchymal and endothelial cells; helper T cells induce inflammation via cytokines
    • Acute humoral rejection: Alloantibodies (against MHC molecules) bind endothelial cells → endothelial injury + intravascular thrombosis → graft destruction
    Chronic Rejection
    • Major cause of transplant failure
    • Develops over months to years
    • Activation of alloreactive T cells → secretion of inflammatory cytokines (e.g., IFN-γ) → continuous inflammation → fibrosis
    • Kidney & heart: vascular occlusion, interstitial fibrosis → organ failure
    • Liver: fibrotic, nonfunctional bile ducts

    Mechanism: Allorecognition

    Type Description Outcome
    Direct Recognition
    • Recipient T cells recognize intact, unprocessed donor MHC molecules on graft cells
    • Donor dendritic cells migrate to draining lymph nodes → present unprocessed allogeneic MHC class I/II to recipient CD8⁺/CD4⁺ T cells
    		Leads to effector T cell differentiation → direct killing of graft cells
    Indirect Recognition
    • Recipient APCs (dendritic cells, macrophages, B cells) process and present donor MHC-derived peptides bound to recipient MHC molecules
    		Activates CD4⁺ Th cells → help B cells produce donor-specific antibodies and activate macrophages → inflammation-mediated injury

    Chain of Events Leading to Graft Rejection

    Step Description
    1 T cell responses initiated in draining lymph nodes
    2 Donor APCs migrate to lymph node → direct MHC allorecognition
    3 Recipient dendritic cells migrate into graft, pick up alloantigens, transport to lymph node → indirect pathway activates helper T and B cells
    4 Naive helper and cytotoxic T lymphocytes encounter alloantigens → proliferation + differentiation into effector cells → migrate back to graft → mediate rejection
    5 Antibodies (donor-specific) contribute via opsonization and complement activation

    5. Hematopoietic Stem Cell Transplant (HSCT) & Graft-vs-Host Disease (GVHD)

    Topic Details
    Overview of HSCT
    • Used to treat hematological cancers (leukemia, lymphoma), immune disorders, blood disorders (sickle cell disease, thalassemia)
    • Progenitor stem cells extracted from peripheral blood, cord blood, or bone marrow
    • Process: extraction → chemotherapy (wipes out immune system) → stem cells treated/stored → reintroduction → isolation → follow-up
    GVHD Definition
    • Donor T cells in the graft attack the recipient (host)
    • More frequent in HSCT than solid organ transplant
    • Solid organs with higher T-cell numbers (liver, lung, small intestine) have higher risk

    Risk Factors for GVHD in HSCT

    Risk Factor Details
    HLA incompatibility Mainly minor histocompatibility antigens
    Patient age Older age increases risk
    Female gender (& multiple pregnancies) Higher risk
    Aggressive host immunosuppression (initiation) Rapid tapering of immunosuppression increases risk
    Stem cell source Peripheral blood > bone marrow > cord blood

    Mechanism of GVHD

    Pathway Events
    CD4-mediated (Th1) pathway
    • Host dendritic cells pick up antigens from various tissues (e.g., skin) → travel to lymph node → present on MHC class II → meet donor helper T cells
    • Activated Th1 cells secrete IL-2 and IFN-γ
    • IFN-γ activates phagocytes (macrophages) → release pro-inflammatory cytokines (TNF-α, IL-1, IL-6) → recruit new immune cells, induce more MHC class II expression on host cells → easier interaction between donor T cells and host APCs
    CD8-mediated pathway
    • Grafted cytotoxic T cells recognize antigens on MHC class I of all nucleated host cells
    • Release perforin and granzymes → form pores → induce apoptosis
    • Also induced via Fas–Fas ligand interaction
    • Actions of helper and cytotoxic T cells result in tissue injury

    Development of GVHD

    Step Description
    1 Donor T cells recognize recipient antigens on dendritic cells → activation and proliferation of donor Th cells
    2 Activated Th cells release cytokines (TNF-α, IL-2, IFN-γ) → activate phagocytes, induce inflammation and apoptosis
    3 Donor CTLs activated upon encountering recipient MHC class I → cell destruction via FAS and perforin/granzymes
    4 Effector phase: expanded CTLs, NK cells, Th cells, inflammatory cytokines cause direct tissue destruction; innate cells (neutrophils, monocytes) contribute
    5 B cells may become involved in progressive disease

    6. Acute vs. Chronic GVHD

    Acute GVHD (within first 3 months post-transplant)

    Induction Short-term, broad immunosuppression at time of transplant

    Periodontal Breakdown Cascade – Comprehensive Notes

    Pathogenesis Overview & Disease Initiation

    Category Key Concept Description & Mechanism Clinical / Pathological Significance
    Definition of Pathogenesis Origin and development of disease Step-by-step process by which etiologic factors cause structural and functional changes in the periodontium Forms the framework for understanding the transition from health → disease
    Sources of Inflammatory Signals
    1. Subgingival microflora: microbial virulence factors
    2. Host immune-inflammatory response
    • Bacteria release noxious substances, but cause limited direct damage
    • Majority of tissue breakdown is due to host response
    		Bacteria initiate and sustain inflammation, but host response causes the bulk of destruction
    Biofilm Shift Health-associated biofilm ↔ Dysbiotic microbial community Change in microbial composition drives pathology Leads to severe tissue inflammation and bone resorption

    Microbial Virulence Factors

    Component Structure / Origin Mechanism of Action Key Features
    Lipopolysaccharide (LPS) Outer membrane of gram-negative bacteria (lipid + polysaccharide) → endotoxin Recognized by toll-like receptors (TLRs) → triggers intracellular events → increased inflammatory mediators (cytokines) and immune cell differentiation
    • Elicits strong immune responses
    • Essential for structural integrity of bacterial cells
    Lipoteichoic Acid (LTA) Component of gram-positive cell walls Stimulates immune responses less potently than LPS Both LPS and LTA: ↑ vasodilation, ↑ vascular permeability, recruit inflammatory cells via chemotaxis, release proinflammatory mediators from leukocytes
    Toll-Like Receptors (TLRs) Family of cell surface molecules; important in innate immune responses Recognize microbe-associated molecular patterns (MAMPs) Interaction triggers intracellular signaling → increased cytokine production and immune cell differentiation

    Bacterial Enzymes & Noxious Products

    Product Source / Nature Effect on Host / Periodontium
    Fatty acids Bacterial metabolic waste
    • May aid P. gingivalis infection via tissue destruction
    • Create nutrient supply by increasing bleeding into pocket
    • Influence cytokine secretion and potentiate inflammatory responses after exposure to LPS, IL-1β, TNF-α
    Proteases Plaque bacteria
    • Break down structural proteins: collagen, elastin, fibronectin
    • Digest proteins → provide peptides for bacterial nutrition
    • Disrupt host responses
    • Compromise tissue integrity
    • Facilitate microbial tissue invasion
    Microbial Invasion Bacteria (cocci, filaments, rods) identified histologically in intercellular spaces of epithelium Acts as a reservoir for reinfection; however, clinical relevance not yet defined → inappropriate to base treatment decisions (e.g., adjunctive systemic antibiotics) on this finding alone

    Host-Derived Inflammatory Mediators

    Mediator Class Type Source Cells Primary Function in Periodontitis
    Cytokines Soluble proteins acting as messengers Neutrophils, macrophages, lymphocytes, fibroblasts, epithelial cells Signal, broadcast, amplify immune responses; regulate immune-inflammatory responses and combat infection; prolonged/excessive production → connective tissue and alveolar bone destruction via induction of fibroblasts and osteoclasts to produce proteolytic enzymes (MMPs)
    Prostanoids (esp. PGE2) Lipid compounds derived from arachidonic acid Macrophages (major), fibroblasts Result in vasodilation; induce cytokine production; induce MMPs and osteoclastic bone resorption → major role in tissue damage characterizing periodontitis
    Matrix Metalloproteinases (MMPs) Zinc/calcium-dependent proteolytic enzymes that degrade ECM molecules (collagen, gelatin, elastin) Neutrophils, macrophages, fibroblasts, epithelial cells, osteoblasts, osteoclasts Degrade all ECM components; MMP-8 and MMP-9 are predominant in periodontitis → degrade type I collagen; inhibited by tetracycline → led to development of antimicrobial formulation of doxycycline as adjunctive treatment using anti-MMP properties

    Proinflammatory Cytokines – IL-1β & TNF-α

    Cytokine Role in Periodontal Disease
    IL-1β Plays a key role in initiation, regulation, and perpetuation of innate immune responses in the periodontium; results in vascular changes and migration of neutrophils; regulates expression of RANKL and OPG
    TNF-α Similar key role as IL-1β in innate immune responses; regulates expression of RANKL and OPG

    Once the immune-inflammatory processes are initiated and the complex cytokine network is established, various inflammatory molecules playing a direct role in periodontium degradation are produced in response to MAMPs and/or host-derived cytokines.

    Linking Pathogenesis to Clinical Signs

    Clinical Sign Pathological Mechanism
    Tooth mobility Loss of attachment between tooth and supporting tissues following breakdown of periodontal ligament inserting fibers and alveolar bone resorption
    Pocket deepening Epithelial cells in sulcular epithelium respond to bacterial products by secreting chemokines to attract neutrophils; persistent bacterial challenge → cellular and fluid infiltration continues → continued tissue damage and collagen depletion → epithelium proliferates apically → pocket deepens and is colonized by subgingival bacteria
    Bleeding on probing Pocket epithelium becomes thin/ulcerated and bleeds more readily
    Attachment loss / increased probing depth Collagen fibers and connective tissue attachments to tooth are destroyed; epithelial cells proliferate apically along root surface; represents increased inflammation severity and apical displacement of inflammatory infiltrate

    Alveolar Bone Resorption – The RANK/RANKL/OPG System

    Component Description Role in Periodontal Disease
    Osteoclasts Cells that resorb bone Stimulated by proinflammatory cytokines (IL-1β, TNF-α, IL-6, PGE2) to resorb bone; alveolar bone retreats from advancing inflammatory front — a protective mechanism, but leads to tooth mobility/loss
    RANK Cell surface receptor expressed by osteoclasts Binding of RANKL to RANK → cell activation and differentiation → mature osteoclasts
    RANKL Produced by: fibroblasts, osteoblasts, chondrocytes, mesenchymal cells, T- and B-lymphocytes Pivotal role in bone resorption; involved in osteoclast differentiation, activation, and survival; IL-1β and TNF-α regulate its expression
    OPG Secreted primarily by: osteoblastic cells, bone marrow stromal cells, fibroblasts Endogenous inhibitor of RANKL; functions as decoy receptor, preventing RANKL binding to RANK → downregulates osteoclastogenesis
    Clinical correlation Patients with advanced periodontitis have higher levels of RANKL and lower levels of OPG than periodontally healthy patients Imbalance favors bone resorption

    Individual Differences & Modulating Factors

    Factor Category Examples Effect
    Genetic variations Individual differences in host response to MAMPs and host-derived cytokines Personalized genetic program controlling how each individual responds to microbial challenge
    Transitory life factors Hormonal imbalances, stress, acquired diseases, medications, smoking Modulate production of cytokines and inflammatory mediators → determine individual's response to microbial challenge and periodontal tissue destruction

    Immune Responses in Periodontal Pathogenesis

    Immune Response Feature Description
    Innate Immunity Definition Mechanical, chemical, and microbiologic barriers preventing pathogen invasion; limited specificity, fixed, does not change/improve during a response or with previous exposure
    Key components Saliva, GCF, epithelial keratinocytes of oral mucosa; commensal microflora
    Activation Triggered when primary defenses are breached → recognition of pathogens and recruitment of neutrophils and complement system
    Recognition system Pattern Recognition Receptors (PRRs) on macrophages and dendritic cells interact with MAMPs
    Outcome Stimulation → inflammation; provides immediate protection; initiates and shapes adaptive immunity
    Adaptive Immunity Definition Antigen-specific targeting for precise pathogen elimination; slower; improves with repeated exposure
    Shift Innate → adaptive as disease becomes established
    Dominant cells in active periodontitis B-cells and plasma cells dominate, linked to pocket formation and progression

    Antigen-Presenting Cells (APCs)

    Component Role
    APCs in periodontium Macrophages, dendritic cells, B-cells
    MHC II expression APCs express MHC class II molecules → capture periodontal pathogens → transport antigens to lymph nodes → activate effector T-cells → generate targeted immune responses
    Requirements for T-cell/B-cell activation Both APCs and cytokine signals that guide T-cell development

    Chronic Inflammation Cycle Summary

    Step Description
    1 Presence of subgingival bacteria drives inflammatory responses
    2 Leukocyte infiltration, inflammatory mediators, and destructive enzymes are released
    3 Connective tissue breakdown occurs
    4 Epithelium breaks down and proliferates in an apical direction → pocket deepens
    5 Pocket becomes colonized by subgingival bacteria
    6 Attempts at effective oral hygiene become more difficult by pocket deepening
    7 Cycle continues → progressive tissue destruction
